£677.6 Million — Inside Manchester United's Complicated Financial Picture

Manchester United generated record revenue in 2025/26, but still recorded a £43 million loss. We break down where the money came from, where it went, and what the numbers mean for the club's future.

By Brandon Williams | September 23, 2026
Hero image — Manchester United financial report

Manchester United generated a record £677.6 million in revenue during the 2025/26 financial year.

And yet, when the books were closed, the club had still recorded a £43 million net loss.

How can one of the biggest football clubs in the world generate hundreds of millions of pounds in revenue and still finish the year in the red?

The answer is more complicated than simply asking how much money Manchester United makes.

The club's latest financial results, covering the year ended June 30, 2026, provide a detailed picture of where United's money came from, where it went, and what its finances could look like heading into the next financial year.

There are genuine signs of improvement. United moved from an £18.4 million operating loss to a £22.6 million operating profit, while employee benefit expenses fell by £11.3 million. Adjusted EBITDA reached a record £216.4 million.

But the club's overall net loss increased from £33 million to £43 million.

So what actually happened?

Where Does United's Money Come From?

United's £677.6 million in revenue came from three main sources: commercial activities, broadcasting, and matchday income.

Commercial: £317.3 Million

Commercial revenue was United's largest source of income, bringing in £317.3 million during the year.

This includes sponsorship, retail, merchandising, apparel and product licensing. However, it was also the one major revenue category that declined, falling 4.8% from £333.3 million the previous year.

Sponsorship revenue fell 14.8% to £160.5 million, while retail, merchandising, apparel and product licensing increased 8.2% to £156.8 million.

In simple terms, United made less money from sponsorships but more from selling its products. The overall decline therefore stands out even as retail performance improved.

Broadcasting: £206.8 Million

Broadcasting was the biggest positive change.

United generated £206.8 million from broadcasting in 2025/26, an increase of £33.9 million, or 19.6%, from the previous year.

The main factor was the club's improved Premier League finish. United finished third, compared with 15th the previous season, significantly increasing Premier League broadcasting income despite United not participating in UEFA competition during the financial year.

That becomes particularly relevant looking ahead. Champions League football has returned for 2026/27, creating another potential source of broadcasting and match-related income.

Matchday: £153.5 Million

United brought in £153.5 million from matchdays in 2025/26, down £6.8 million, or 4.2%, from the previous year.

The decline does not necessarily mean supporters were spending less at Old Trafford. United hosted 10 fewer home matches than the previous season, which was the primary reason for the decrease. The club also reported strong demand for general admission and hospitality offerings.

The three revenue streams can therefore be summarized simply:

Commercial: £317.3m — down 4.8%

Broadcasting: £206.8m — up 19.6%

Matchday: £153.5m — down 4.2%

Together, they produced United's £677.6 million in total revenue. The record headline number was therefore not the result of every part of the business growing. Broadcasting did most of the heavy lifting, while commercial and matchday revenue both declined.

If Manchester United brought in £677.6 million, where did all of that money go?

Where Does the Money Go?

Running Manchester United costs hundreds of millions of pounds every year. United's total operating expenses were £701.9 million in 2025/26, a decrease of £31.8 million, or 4.3%, from the previous year.

That reduction shows that United did make progress in controlling its operating costs, but the individual numbers tell a more complicated story.

£302 Million on Employee Costs

The largest single expense was employee benefit expenses, which came to £302 million.

The figure fell by £11.3 million, or 3.6%, from the previous year. United attributed the reduction primarily to changes in the men's first-team squad and savings associated with headcount-reduction programmes.

£302 million still represents roughly 45% of the club's total revenue.

The £211.8 Million Player Cost

One of the most misunderstood numbers in the accounts is amortisation.

United recorded £211.8 million in player amortisation during 2025/26, an increase of £15.4 million, or 7.8%, from the previous year. The club said the increase was due to continued investment in the first-team playing squad.

But £211.8 million does not mean United physically paid £211.8 million to other clubs during the year.

Transfer fees are generally recognised as an accounting expense over the length of a player's contract. For example, a £50 million transfer on a five-year contract would generally produce an accounting expense of roughly £10 million per year rather than £50 million in the first season.

That process is called amortisation.

At June 30, 2026, United had £452.3 million of unamortised player registrations on its balance sheet. The financial consequences of previous transfer deals can therefore continue to appear in the accounts over the length of those contracts.

Other Costs

United recorded £159.2 million in other operating expenses, a decrease of £11.2 million, or 6.6%, from the previous year.

The club attributed this reduction primarily to improved operating efficiency and lower matchday costs resulting from hosting 10 fewer home matches.

Depreciation and impairment added another £20.6 million, while £8.2 million in exceptional costs were recorded, primarily related to the departure of former men's first-team head coach Ruben Amorim and final costs associated with restructuring programmes.

United generated £677.6 million in revenue and incurred £701.9 million in operating expenses. But even that does not fully explain the final £43 million loss.

£22.6m Profit, £43m Loss: How?

After accounting for its operating expenses, United recorded an operating profit of £22.6 million in 2025/26, compared with an operating loss of £18.4 million a year earlier.

So, at the operating level, United had moved from losing money to making money. But that was not the final number.

United ultimately recorded a £43.0 million net loss.

The Cost of Financing Manchester United

The biggest difference came from net finance costs.

United recorded £69.6 million in net finance costs during 2025/26, compared with £21.2 million the previous year.

Finance costs include financial effects associated with the club's borrowings as well as foreign-exchange movements.

United reported a £10 million unrealised foreign-exchange loss on unhedged US-dollar borrowings, compared with a £22.9 million unrealised foreign-exchange gain the previous year.

The Numbers Tell the Story

Revenue: £677.6m

Operating profit: +£22.6m

Net finance costs: -£69.6m

Loss before tax: -£47.0m

Income tax credit: +£4.0m

Final net loss: £43.0m

The distinction between operating profit and net loss is crucial. Looking only at the £43 million loss could suggest that every aspect of United's business got worse. That's not what the accounts show.

United's operating result improved by more than £40 million. Employee costs fell. Total operating expenses fell. Adjusted EBITDA increased from £182.8 million to £216.4 million.

Manchester United's operating performance improved, but the club remained loss-making overall.

Cash, Borrowing, and the Stadium

The income statement tells us how Manchester United performed over the financial year. The balance sheet and cash-flow figures give us another perspective: what the club had on hand, what it owed, and where it was putting its money.

Cash Generation

Manchester United ended the 2025/26 financial year with £67.2 million in cash and cash equivalents, down £18.9 million from the previous year.

However, the club generated £178.7 million of net cash from operating activities, compared with £72.7 million in 2024/25.

So while the cash balance decreased, the club's operations generated substantially more cash than they had the year before. That cash was then being used across the business, including investment in the playing squad and infrastructure.

Borrowing

At June 30, United had £577.6 million in non-current borrowings, compared with £471.9 million a year earlier.

The club also had £111.4 million in current borrowings, including accrued interest. The majority of the current borrowing related to its revolving credit facility.

United's US-dollar-denominated non-current borrowings increased from $650 million to $775 million following the refinancing of its senior secured notes.

Those figures matter because financing has a cost, as reflected by the £69.6 million in net finance costs recorded during the year.

And Then There's the Stadium

United also began taking concrete financial steps toward its proposed new stadium.

During 2025/26, United spent £85.9 million on property, plant and equipment. Of that, £63.5 million was spent acquiring land connected to the club's ambition to build a new 100,000-seat stadium.

United did not spend £63.5 million constructing the new stadium during this financial year. The money was used to acquire land for the proposed project.

The financial question is therefore not simply whether a new stadium could generate more revenue. It is how the eventual project will be financed, what returns it will generate, and how much financial strain it could place on the club while it is being built.

For now, the much larger financial commitment lies ahead.

What Happens Next?

The 2025/26 financial results leave Manchester United in a complicated position.

Revenue reached a record £677.6 million. Adjusted EBITDA reached a record £216.4 million. United moved from an £18.4 million operating loss to a £22.6 million operating profit, while employee costs fell by £11.3 million. The club also generated £178.7 million in cash from operating activities.

But the club still recorded a £43 million net loss. Net finance costs increased sharply, borrowing remained substantial, cash on hand declined, and the club has begun committing money toward a proposed new stadium.

Champions League Changes the Equation

United's return to the Champions League is the most immediate opportunity to increase revenue.

The men's first team finished third in the Premier League in 2025/26, securing qualification for the 2026/27 Champions League. United had no UEFA competition during the financial year covered by these accounts.

The club is forecasting £740 million to £760 million in revenue for fiscal 2027, compared with £677.6 million in 2026. It is also forecasting adjusted EBITDA of £205 million to £225 million.

The midpoint of the 2027 revenue guidance is £750 million — roughly £72 million above this year's record.

The midpoint of the adjusted EBITDA guidance is £215 million, almost exactly in line with the £216.4 million United generated this year.

United is therefore forecasting substantially more revenue without forecasting a significant increase in adjusted EBITDA. That will be worth watching as Champions League football returns and the club's costs change alongside its additional revenue.

What Do These Accounts Actually Tell Us?

There is no single number that explains Manchester United's financial position.

The £677.6 million revenue figure shows the scale of the club's business. The £216.4 million adjusted EBITDA shows the strength of its operating performance before certain costs. The £22.6 million operating profit shows that United's operating result moved back into positive territory.

But the £43 million net loss shows that those improvements have not yet translated into an overall profit. The £69.6 million in net finance costs shows how significant the club's financial structure remains, while the £63.5 million spent acquiring stadium land shows that another major financial commitment is beginning to take shape.

Together, the figures show a club generating enormous amounts of revenue and making measurable progress in controlling operating costs, while still facing substantial financing costs and preparing for major future investment.

The next financial year will provide another test. Champions League football has returned, United expects revenue to rise again, and the club has begun taking concrete steps toward its proposed new stadium.

But increased revenue is only part of the equation.

The bigger question is whether Manchester United can turn that revenue into sustainable profit and cash generation while managing its borrowing, player costs, and the enormous investment required for its plans.

For a club generating nearly £700 million a year, perhaps the most important question is the simplest one:

How much of it can Manchester United actually keep?
