£677.6 Million — Inside Manchester United's Complicated Financial Picture

Manchester United generated record revenue in 2025/26, but still recorded a £43 million loss. We break down where the money came from, where it went, and what the numbers mean for the club's future.

By The Red Thread | September 23, 2026
Hero image — Manchester United financial report

Manchester United generated a record £677.6 million in revenue during the 2025/26 financial year.

And yet, when the books were closed, the club had still recorded a £43 million net loss.

How can one of the biggest football clubs in the world generate hundreds of millions of pounds in revenue and still finish the year in the red?

The answer is unfortunately more complicated than that.

The club's latest financial results, covering the year ended June 30, 2026, provide a detailed picture of where United's money came from, where it went, and what its finances could look like heading into the next financial year.

There are genuine signs of improvement. United moved from an £18.4 million operating loss to a £22.6 million operating profit, while employee benefit expenses fell by £11.3 million. Adjusted EBITDA reached a record £216.4 million.

But the club's overall net loss increased from £33 million to £43 million.

So what actually happened?

Where Does United's Money Come From?

To understand Manchester United's financial position, the first step is understanding where the money actually comes from.

United's £677.6 million in revenue came from three main sources: commercial activities, broadcasting, and matchday income. Each tells us something different about the club's business.

Commercial: £317.3 Million

Commercial revenue was United's largest source of income, bringing in £317.3 million during the year.

This includes sponsorship, retail, merchandising, apparel and product licensing.

However, this was also the one major revenue category that declined. Commercial revenue fell 4.8%, or £16 million, from £333.3 million the previous year.

Sponsorship revenue fell 14.8% to £160.5 million, while retail, merchandising, apparel and product licensing increased 8.2% to £156.8 million.

In simple terms, United made less money from sponsorships but more from selling its products.

That distinction matters because commercial revenue remains the club's largest source of income. The overall decline therefore stands out even though retail performance improved.

Broadcasting: £206.8 Million

Broadcasting was the biggest positive change.

United generated £206.8 million from broadcasting in 2025/26, an increase of £33.9 million, or 19.6%, from the previous year.

The main factor was the club's improved Premier League finish.

United finished third in the Premier League, compared with 15th the previous season. That significantly increased Premier League broadcasting income, although United did not participate in UEFA competition during the financial year.

United therefore managed to increase broadcasting revenue substantially despite playing no European football during the season.

That is particularly relevant when looking ahead. Champions League football has returned to Old Trafford for 2026/27, creating another potential source of broadcasting and match-related income.

Matchday: £153.5 Million

The third source is matchday revenue — money generated from actually hosting games at Old Trafford.

United brought in £153.5 million from matchdays in 2025/26, down £6.8 million, or 4.2%, from the previous year.

But the decline does not necessarily mean supporters were spending less at Old Trafford.

United hosted 10 fewer home matches than the previous season, which was the primary reason for the decrease. The club also reported strong demand for general admission and hospitality offerings.

A lower annual matchday figure can therefore be caused simply by having fewer opportunities to sell tickets, hospitality packages and other matchday products.

The Bigger Picture

The three revenue streams can be summarized simply:

Commercial: £317.3m — down 4.8%

Broadcasting: £206.8m — up 19.6%

Matchday: £153.5m — down 4.2%

Together, they produced United's £677.6 million in total revenue.

The record headline number was therefore not the result of every part of the business growing. Broadcasting did most of the heavy lifting, while commercial and matchday revenue both declined.

That brings us to the other side of the equation.

If Manchester United brought in £677.6 million, where did all of that money go?

Where Does the Money Go?

Generating £677.6 million is only half of the story.

Running Manchester United costs hundreds of millions of pounds every year, and the financial results show how much of that revenue is absorbed by employees, player costs, and the day-to-day operation of the club.

United's total operating expenses were £701.9 million in 2025/26, a decrease of £31.8 million, or 4.3%, from the previous year.

That reduction is significant because it shows that United did make progress in controlling its operating costs.

But the individual numbers tell a more compelling story.

£302 Million on Employee Costs

The largest single expense was employee benefit expenses, which came to £302 million.

The figure fell by £11.3 million, or 3.6%, from the previous year. United attributed the reduction primarily to changes in the men's first-team squad and savings associated with headcount-reduction programmes.

In other words, United spent less on its employees than it did a year earlier.

But £302 million is still an enormous annual expense, representing roughly 45% of the club's total revenue.

The £211.8 Million Player Cost

Then there is one of the most misunderstood numbers in the accounts: amortisation.

United recorded £211.8 million in player amortisation during 2025/26, an increase of £15.4 million, or 7.8%, from the previous year. The club said the increase was due to continued investment in the first-team playing squad.

But £211.8 million does not mean United physically paid £211.8 million to other clubs during the year.

Instead, transfer fees are generally recognised as an accounting expense over the length of a player's contract.

For example, if a club signs a player for £50 million on a five-year contract, the accounting cost is generally spread across those five years rather than recorded as a £50 million expense in the first season.

That process is called amortisation.

United's £211.8 million therefore reflects the accounting cost of transfer fees associated with its current and previous recruitment.

And there is still a substantial amount left to be recognised in future years. At June 30, 2026, United had £452.3 million of unamortised player registrations on its balance sheet.

This is important because transfer spending does not simply disappear from the accounts when a player signs. The financial consequences of those deals can continue to appear over the length of the player's contract.

Other Costs

United also recorded £159.2 million in other operating expenses, a decrease of £11.2 million, or 6.6%, from the previous year.

The club attributed this reduction primarily to improved operating efficiency and lower matchday costs resulting from hosting 10 fewer home matches.

Depreciation and impairment added another £20.6 million.

There were also £8.2 million in exceptional costs, primarily related to the departure of former men's first-team head coach Ruben Amorim and final costs associated with the club's restructuring programmes.

Taken together, the scale of United's annual expenditure becomes clear.

The club generated £677.6 million in revenue.

It incurred £701.9 million in operating expenses.

Yet even that does not fully explain the final £43 million loss.

Because once the club's operating performance is calculated, another major cost enters the picture.

Finance costs.

£22.6m Profit, £43m Loss: How?

After accounting for its operating expenses, United recorded an operating profit of £22.6 million in 2025/26.

A year earlier, the club had recorded an operating loss of £18.4 million.

So, at the operating level, United had moved from losing money to making money.

But that was not the final number.

United ultimately recorded a £43.0 million net loss.

So what happened between those two figures?

The Cost of Financing Manchester United

The biggest difference came from net finance costs.

United recorded £69.6 million in net finance costs during 2025/26, compared with £21.2 million the previous year.

This is an important distinction: finance costs are not simply another name for footballing expenses.

They include the financial effects associated with the club's borrowings as well as foreign-exchange movements.

United reported a £10 million unrealised foreign-exchange loss on unhedged US-dollar borrowings, compared with a £22.9 million unrealised foreign-exchange gain the previous year.

That swing alone significantly affected the comparison between the two years.

The Numbers Tell the Story

The journey from operating performance to the final result can be simplified:

Revenue: £677.6m

Operating profit: +£22.6m

Net finance costs: -£69.6m

Loss before tax: -£47.0m

Income tax credit: +£4.0m

Final net loss: £43.0m

That distinction between operating profit and net loss is crucial.

If someone looked only at the £43 million loss, they could conclude that every aspect of United's business got worse.

That's not what the accounts show.

United's operating result improved by more than £40 million. Employee costs fell. Total operating expenses fell. Adjusted EBITDA increased from £182.8 million to £216.4 million.

The problem is that those improvements did not eliminate the costs sitting below operating profit.

A Better Operating Result — But Still a Loss

The result is therefore more complicated than the headline £43 million loss suggests.

Manchester United generated more revenue.

Its operating result improved dramatically.

Its employee costs fell.

Its adjusted EBITDA increased.

And yet the club still finished the financial year with a larger net loss than the year before.

Manchester United's operating performance improved, but the club remained loss-making overall.

That is the central tension running through these accounts.

And that brings us to another question: how much financial pressure is sitting on the club's balance sheet?

Cash, Borrowing, and the Stadium

The income statement tells us how Manchester United performed over the financial year. The balance sheet and cash-flow figures give us another perspective: what the club had on hand, what it owed, and where it was putting its money.

Cash Generation

Manchester United ended the 2025/26 financial year with £67.2 million in cash and cash equivalents, down £18.9 million from the previous year.

However, looking only at the cash balance would give an incomplete picture.

United generated £178.7 million of net cash from operating activities during the year, compared with £72.7 million in 2024/25.

So while the cash balance decreased, the club's operations generated substantially more cash than they had the year before.

That cash was then being used across the business, including investment in the playing squad and infrastructure.

Borrowing

At June 30, United had £577.6 million in non-current borrowings, compared with £471.9 million a year earlier.

The club also had £111.4 million in current borrowings, including accrued interest. The majority of the current borrowing related to its revolving credit facility.

United's US-dollar-denominated non-current borrowings increased from $650 million to $775 million following the refinancing of its senior secured notes.

These figures matter because financing has a cost.

As the previous section showed, United's net finance costs reached £69.6 million during the year.

The financial structure of the club therefore remains an important part of understanding its overall profitability.

And Then There's the Stadium

Perhaps the most significant long-term development in these accounts is the beginning of United's proposed new stadium project.

During 2025/26, United spent £85.9 million on property, plant and equipment. Of that, £63.5 million was spent acquiring land connected to the club's ambition to build a new 100,000-seat stadium.

The financial question is therefore not simply whether a new stadium could generate more revenue.

It is how the eventual project will be financed, what returns it will generate and how much financial strain it could place on the club while it is being built.

For now, the financial results show that the project has moved beyond an idea on paper. United has acquired the land, and £63.5 million of that expenditure is already reflected in the 2025/26 accounts.

The much larger financial commitment, however, lies ahead.

What Happens Next?

The 2025/26 financial results leave Manchester United in a complicated position.

There are clear signs that the club's underlying operations improved.

Revenue reached a record £677.6 million. Adjusted EBITDA reached a record £216.4 million. United moved from an £18.4 million operating loss to a £22.6 million operating profit, while employee costs fell by £11.3 million. The club also generated £178.7 million in cash from operating activities.

But the club still recorded a £43 million net loss.

Net finance costs increased sharply, borrowing remained substantial, cash on hand declined, and the club has begun committing money toward a proposed new stadium.

So what happens next?

Champions League Changes the Equation

United's return to the Champions League is perhaps the most immediate opportunity to increase revenue.

The men's first team finished third in the Premier League in 2025/26, securing qualification for the 2026/27 Champions League. United had no UEFA competition during the financial year covered by these accounts.

The club is forecasting £740 million to £760 million in revenue for fiscal 2027, compared with £677.6 million in 2026. It is also forecasting adjusted EBITDA of £205 million to £225 million.

That means United expects another significant increase in revenue.

But there is an interesting detail in that forecast.

The midpoint of the 2027 revenue guidance is £750 million — roughly £72 million above this year's record.

The midpoint of the adjusted EBITDA guidance is £215 million, almost exactly in line with the £216.4 million United generated this year.

So the club is forecasting substantially more revenue without forecasting a significant increase in adjusted EBITDA.

That will be worth watching as the Champions League returns and the club's costs change alongside its additional revenue.

What Do These Accounts Actually Tell Us?

There is no single number that explains Manchester United's financial position.

The £677.6 million revenue figure shows the scale of the club's business.

The £216.4 million adjusted EBITDA shows how much the operating business generated before certain costs.

The £22.6 million operating profit shows that United's underlying operating result moved back into positive territory.

But the £43 million net loss shows that those improvements have not yet translated into an overall profit.

The £69.6 million in net finance costs shows how significant the club's financial structure remains.

And the £63.5 million spent acquiring stadium land shows that another major financial commitment is beginning to take shape.

None of these figures should be viewed in isolation.

Together, they show a club generating enormous amounts of revenue and making measurable progress in controlling operating costs, while still facing substantial financing costs and preparing for major future investment.

The next financial year will provide another test.

Champions League football has returned. United expects revenue to rise again. And the club has begun taking concrete steps toward its proposed new stadium.

But increased revenue is only part of the equation.

The bigger question is whether Manchester United can turn that revenue into sustainable profit and cash generation while managing its borrowing, player costs, and the enormous investment required for its plans.

For a club generating nearly £700 million a year, perhaps the most important question is the simplest one:

How much of it can Manchester United actually keep?
